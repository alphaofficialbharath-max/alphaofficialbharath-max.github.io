Touch typing basics: the home row and where every finger goes

Touch typing means typing without looking at the keyboard, by touch. It is a map, not a talent - and the map fits on one screen. Learn it once here, then practise it on the free test.

What touch typing actually is

Instead of hunting each letter with two fingers, a touch typist keeps all eight fingers resting on one home base and reaches out with the correct finger for every key. Because the path for every letter stays the same forever, fingers memorise distances and speed follows automatically - most learners double their speed within weeks without stress.

The home row

Rest your left fingers on A, S, D and F, and your right fingers on J, K, L and the semicolon. Thumbs hover over the space bar. This middle row of letters is the home row: the place every hand returns to between words. If your fingers drift, come back to these eight keys first - everything else in touch typing is measured from here.

The bumps on F and J

Feel the small raised bars on F and J. They are not damage - they are anchors. Without looking, you should be able to slide each index finger onto its bump and know the whole hand is seated correctly. Good typists touch these bumps a hundred times an hour without thinking about it.

Which finger owns which keys

  • Left pinky - Q, A, Z, shift, tab.
  • Left ring - W, S, X.
  • Left middle - E, D, C.
  • Left index - R, T, F, G, V, B and number 4 and 5.
  • Right index - Y, U, H, J, N, M and number 6 and 7.
  • Right middle - I, K, comma.
  • Right ring - O, L, full stop.
  • Right pinky - P, semicolon, slash, apostrophe, enter and the right shift.
  • Thumbs - the space bar, whichever thumb feels natural.

Notice the diagonal rule: each finger stretches up-right and down-left in a straight line from its home key. Once you see the diagonals, the map stops feeling crowded.

Space, shift and capitals

Capitals come from the shift key on the opposite hand - left shift for right-hand letters like "J", right shift for left-hand letters like "T". Leaning this rule early keeps you from the bad habit of rolling one hand across the keyboard, which is where most beginners lose their first weeks of progress.

How long until it feels natural

Expect the first week to feel slower than your old two-finger style - that is normal and it is a sign the map is loading. Around week two to three, comfort arrives; by week four to six most steady learners pass the speed they had before. The only people who fail are the ones who quit during week one, so keep the daily session short enough that you never dread it.

A simple first-week routine

  1. Set your hands on the home row and read the finger map above once.
  2. Type the sentence "a sad lad had a flask" three times, keeping eyes on the screen.
  3. Take the free typing test in quote mode for five minutes, accuracy over speed.
  4. Log your WPM and accuracy; repeat the whole thing tomorrow.
